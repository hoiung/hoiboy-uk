Do you consciously step and move in dance, or do you move for the sake of moving?

What does it even mean to be consciously moving?

Are you paying attention to each weight transfer, and how? Are you projecting first, before each step?

These are the questions you should be asking. Especially when you are [training alone](/blogs/what-to-practice-zouk-solo/). Even more so when you are dancing with a partner in class. And most of all during a social.

My greatest belief is that you should be conscious of every movement and every step, and how it is done. The idea that muscle memory equals dancing is, to me, completely bullshit, and it is what creates mediocre dancers compared to what true dancers are.

The habits we learn through constant training are not something you automate then fire and forget. Every conscious decision, every minor touch and change, every breath should be checked.

While we are here, let me bust the term itself. Your muscles have no memory. None. They have no brain, they store nothing, they contract when they are told to and that is the whole job. What everyone calls muscle memory actually lives in your nervous system, in your brain and your spinal cord. It is a pathway that got faster. It is not a movement filed away somewhere safe and finished. That is exactly why it is so dangerous to trust it, because a faster pathway will carry a bad movement just as happily as a good one.

I'll own this one before anyone else points it out. Go back through my older dance blogs and you will find me using the term myself. I think I used it just for the sake of using it, the way everybody does. I've known better for quite a while now, though I honestly couldn't tell you whether that was before or after I wrote those posts. The old words are staying exactly where they are. I've just put a note on them.

No, I am not saying you need to be manually breathing. Air in, air out, activate lungs, deactivate lungs. No. You can take control of it whenever you want, deeper or shallower, calmer, longer. But by norm it happens naturally without you. What I am saying is you need to be constantly monitoring all of these, so you are ready to manipulate or change them as and when you want.

I have been banging on about this for years. Back in the [5 Stages of Learning](/blogs/5-stages-learning-social-dance-movements/) I wrote that there is a misconception that dance is automated, that you teach your body to do something without thinking so you are free to focus on the music or on feeling your partner. It was not true then and it is not true now.

Yes, that is like a thousand things to think about.

## Humans Can Not Multi-Task, and You Are No Different

We humans suck at multitasking. If you think you are good at it, or you buy the idea that women are better at it than men, then you are probably terrible at it, overconfident, and prone to making mistakes without realising.

Our brain and our body were not designed for multitasking. That is why we suck at it. The sooner you accept that, the easier this will be to understand and to learn how it is actually done.

Accepted?

To make sure you understand, repeat after me.

"Humans can not multi-task, and I am no different."

You can try argue with me about this, but you better bring some substantial proof. Go do some research. And if you still insist you can multi-task, then you must be a special kind of stupid, or a new breed of super human with 2 brains, like how an octopus has 3 hearts to assist tasks in parallel.

I have met my fair share of delusional people over the years. Several who insist they have photographic memories, when they absolutely don't. lol.

Good. Now you have this understanding and myths busted, you have a better start to improving faster.

## What Looks Like Magic Is Just Fast Switching

The reason humans can be efficient comes down to being able to do singular tasks well, and to do them fast, through practice. What may seem or appear to be magic, what looks like multitasking but is not, is how quickly we can singularly access our memories and our movement training through spinal cord coordination. Building those synapses and connections to increase the bandwidth, so we can access and process the information quicker.

Being a bit of a computer geek, there is a name for this. Time-slicing. One core, one task at a time, switching so fast it looks like everything is happening at once. Tasks get queued by priority, broken up, slotted back in, each with its own check-in point. Nothing is actually simultaneous. It just looks that way.

So imagine having to think about all of this at once:

- Which basic step to use.
- Applying techniques on top of the movement.
- Evaluating your body.
- Evaluating your partner's body.
- Dance floor safety checks. Is there any danger around?
- Musicality, processing ahead of time what is coming, and are you on time.
- Where is your weight.
- Where is your partner's weight.
- All your senses. Sight, hearing, touch, pain, and so on.
- And much much more, because the list can go on depending on what level you are at.

And here is the important part. The reason so many people cannot hold all of that at once is that those checks are not fully developed in their brain yet. With that much to juggle, it is easy to forget them. I wrote about this years ago in [Understanding Dance Growth, Plateaux and Bad Habits](/blogs/understanding-dance-growth-plateaux-bad-habits/), that at the early stage our body and mind are not proficient at juggling all the different aspects at once, so we overthink every detail and try to apply it all at the same time.

So the trick isn't applying it all at the same time. It's a step by step process, and a reminder to work through them one at a time, because like we said, we can't multi-task.

This is exactly why training, repetition and exploration are so important. Your brain prioritises the information it uses more often. It is all process driven. The more you use a skill, the sharper it gets, and the quicker you can access it.

## Why This Theory Matters

I feel this is an important theory to understand, because it will humble you. You learn to appreciate and respect the amount of training, learning and hard work it takes to learn how to dance, even to a moderate level.

There is research pointing the same way too. A long-running study out of the Einstein Aging Study in New York followed people over 75 and found dancing was the only physical activity of the lot linked to a lower risk of dementia, Alzheimer's included. Not cycling. Not swimming. Not golf. Dancing. Now that is a link and not proof, and the researchers are careful to say so. But it makes sense to me. You will have built so many connections in there, it will take a lot longer to wear them all down. lol. Logic!

There is even a name for it. Cognitive reserve. Build enough of the stuff and your brain copes for longer.

It also explains why the shortcut never works. Train it until it goes autonomous and you have not saved yourself anything, but congratulations you have just built [bad habits you will have to undo later](/blogs/understanding-dance-growth-plateaux-bad-habits/).

I strongly believe every movement in dance should be done consciously, and should not be trained to be autonomous or subconscious. It needs clear intent. Why, what, when, how.

Only by aiming for this can you aim for a much higher mastery of the art of dancing.
